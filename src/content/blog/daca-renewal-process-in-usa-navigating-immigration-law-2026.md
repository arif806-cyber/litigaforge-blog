## Eligibility Criteria for DACA Renewal in 2026

To be eligible for DACA renewal in 2026, applicants must meet specific criteria that largely mirror the initial DACA requirements, with an emphasis on maintaining continuous eligibility since their last approval. The core requirements, as outlined in the USCIS policy guidance and 8 C.F.R. Part 274a, include:

1.  **Continuous Residence:** The applicant must have continuously resided in the United States since the date they submitted their most recent DACA request that was approved. Brief, casual, and innocent departures from the United States do not break continuous residence.
2.  **No Serious Criminal Record:** Applicants must not have been convicted of a felony, a significant misdemeanor, or three or more other misdemeanors. A “significant misdemeanor” is defined as an offense for which the maximum term of imprisonment authorized is one year or less but greater than five days, and the individual was sentenced to time in custody for more than 90 days, or the offense involves domestic violence, sexual abuse or exploitation, burglary, unlawful possession or use of a firearm, drug distribution or trafficking, or driving under the influence (DUI). This is a critical component, and any criminal history should be reviewed by an attorney.
3.  **Not a Threat to National Security or Public Safety:** The applicant must not otherwise pose a threat to national security or public safety.
4.  **Entry Before Age 16:** The applicant must have been under the age of 16 when they entered the United States.
5.  **Born After June 15, 1981:** The applicant must have been born on or after June 15, 1981.

It is crucial for applicants to review their personal circumstances carefully against these criteria. Any changes in criminal history, travel history, or other personal information since the last DACA approval could impact eligibility. USCIS maintains the discretion to deny renewal requests based on adverse information. Individuals who have had a prior DACA approval revoked or terminated should seek legal counsel, as their path to renewal may be more complex. Moreover, USCIS may request additional evidence to verify continuous residence or to clarify any discrepancies in an applicant's history. [Understanding](/blog/usa-lemon-law-2026-understanding-your-vehicle-return-rights) and meticulously addressing each of these points is key to a successful renewal application.

## Understanding Processing Times and Preventing Lapses in Status

One of the most critical aspects of the DACA renewal process for 2026 is managing processing times to prevent a lapse in deferred action status and employment authorization. A lapse can have serious consequences, including potential loss of employment, inability to renew a driver's license, and an increased risk of encountering immigration enforcement.

**USCIS Processing Times:**
USCIS processing times for DACA renewals can fluctuate significantly based on various factors, including the volume of applications, staffing levels, and policy changes. While there isn't a guaranteed timeframe, USCIS generally advises applying for renewal between **120 and 150 days** (approximately 4 to 5 months) before your current DACA and EAD expire. This window provides sufficient time for processing and allows for potential requests for evidence (RFEs) without jeopardizing your status.

**Checking Processing Times:**
Applicants can check current processing times on the USCIS website. Navigate to the “Check Case Processing Times” tool and select Form I-821D and the appropriate service center. While these times are estimates, they provide a general idea of how long your application might take.

**Consequences of a Lapse:**
*   **Loss of Employment Authorization:** If your EAD expires before your renewal is approved, you will legally be unable to work. This can lead to job termination and financial hardship.
*   **Loss of Deferred Action:** Without deferred action, you are no longer protected from deportation and could be considered unlawfully present in the U.S. This significantly increases your vulnerability to immigration enforcement actions.
*   **Inability to Renew Driver's License:** Many states link driver's license eligibility to valid immigration status and work authorization. A lapse can prevent you from renewing your license.
*   **Impact on Future Applications:** While not automatically disqualifying, a lapse in DACA status can raise questions in future immigration applications and may require additional explanation.

**Preventing a Lapse:**
1.  **Mark Your Calendar:** Set multiple reminders for your DACA and EAD expiration dates. Calculate your 150-day and 120-day windows for application submission.
2.  **Start Early:** Begin gathering documents and completing forms well in advance of the 150-day mark. This allows time to resolve any issues or obtain missing information.
3.  **Submit Timely:** Do not wait until the last minute. Submit your complete application within the recommended 120-150 day window.
4.  **Monitor Your Case:** Regularly check your case status online using the receipt number provided by USCIS. If you don't receive a receipt notice within a few weeks of mailing, contact USCIS.
5.  **Respond Promptly to RFEs:** If USCIS sends a Request for Evidence (RFE), respond immediately and thoroughly with all requested information. Delays in responding can significantly prolong processing or lead to denial.

By diligently adhering to these timelines and monitoring your application, you can significantly reduce the risk of a lapse in your DACA status and employment authorization.

> **Key takeaway:** Submit DACA renewals 120-150 days before expiration to prevent a lapse in status, checking USCIS processing times and responding promptly to any RFEs.
