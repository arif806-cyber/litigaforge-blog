# Hindu Succession Act Daughters Share in Ancestral Property 2026 India

Since the 2005 amendment, daughters enjoy equal coparcenary rights in ancestral property under the Hindu Succession Act. This article explains what that means and how to enforce it.

## Historical Background and the 2005 Amendment

The Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (HSA) originally granted coparcenary rights only to male members of a Hindu Undivided Family (HUF). Section 6 of the Act defined a coparcener as a male descendant of a common ancestor who possessed a right by birth. Women were excluded, limiting their inheritance to a share as a legal heir under Section 14, which often resulted in a smaller portion of the ancestral property.

In 2005, Parliament enacted an amendment through the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, inserting a new clause in Section 6. The amendment states that a daughter shall be treated as a coparcener in the same manner as a son. This change was intended to promote gender equality and align inheritance laws with constitutional guarantees under Article 14 and Article 15 of the Indian Constitution. The amendment became effective on 9 September 2005 and applies to all Hindu families across India.

The practical impact is profound: a daughter now acquires a birthright interest in the HUF property, can demand a partition, and is liable for the same debts as male coparceners. However, the rights are subject to certain conditions, such as the existence of a valid HUF and the absence of a prior partition. Understanding these nuances helps daughters protect their rightful share and avoid disputes.

Key points:
1. Section 6 (as amended) now includes daughters as coparceners.
2. The amendment is retrospective for daughters born after 9 September 2005.
3. Daughters acquire a right by birth, not by testament.
4. The right is enforceable in civil courts under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.
5. Failure to recognize a daughter's rights may lead to legal action for partition and damages.

> **Key takeaway:** Daughters born after 9 Sep 2005 are coparceners with equal rights to ancestral property.

## Understanding Coparcenary Rights Under Section 6 and Section 13

Section 6 of the HSA (as amended) defines a coparcener as a person who acquires a right by birth in a Hindu Undivided Family. The inclusion of daughters means they acquire a right of survivorship, similar to sons. This right is a *jus accrescendi* – the share of a deceased coparcener accrues to the surviving coparceners.

Section 13 outlines the mode of partition. A coparcener, including a daughter, may demand a partition at any time, and the property must be divided equally among all coparceners. The court follows the principle of *equal division*, unless a valid family settlement exists under Section 23. If a daughter seeks partition, the court will assess the value of the HUF assets, including immovable property, agricultural land, and financial assets, and allocate an equal share.

Practical steps for a daughter:
1. **Identify herself as a coparcener** – gather birth certificates, family tree documents, and any HUF registration records.
2. **Serve a notice of partition** – under Order VI Rule 2 of the CPC, a formal notice must be served to all coparceners.
3. **Valuation of assets** – engage a certified valuer to assess the market value of the property.
4. **File a partition suit** – if amicable settlement fails, file a suit under Section 61 of the CPC in the appropriate civil court.
5. **Enforce the decree** – once the court orders partition, the daughter can claim her share, and the registrar can update land records.

Penalties for non-compliance are not criminal but civil. The court may award interest on delayed payments under Section 39 of the CPC and may order costs against the resisting coparceners. This ensures that daughters receive timely justice.

In summary, Section 6 grants the right, while Section 13 provides the mechanism for exercising it.

## Impact of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita on Inheritance Disputes

Effective 1 July 2024, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaced the Indian Penal Code for criminal matters. While inheritance disputes are primarily civil, certain actions—such as intimidation, coercion, or illegal possession—may attract criminal liability under the BNS.

Relevant provisions include:
- **Section 506 BNS (Criminal Intimidation)** – formerly IPC Section 506. If a coparcener threatens a daughter to relinquish her share, the offender may face imprisonment up to three years and a fine.
- **Section 420 BNS (Cheating)** – formerly IPC Section 420. Misrepresenting the HUF assets to deprive a daughter of her share constitutes cheating.
- **Section 441 BNS (Criminal Trespass)** – formerly IPC Section 441, applicable if a male coparcener unlawfully occupies a portion of property meant for the daughter.

These sections provide a deterrent against unlawful conduct. A daughter can file a criminal complaint under the BNS alongside a civil partition suit. The procedural steps are governed by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) for criminal procedure, specifically **Section 154 BNSS** (formerly CrPC Section 154) for registering an FIR.

The synergy between BNS and the HSA strengthens enforcement: civil courts can order partition, while criminal courts can penalize coercive behavior. This dual approach ensures that daughters' rights are protected both substantively and procedurally.

## Procedural Guide: Filing a Partition Suit for Daughters

A partition suit is the most common legal route for a daughter to enforce her coparcenary rights. The following step‑by‑step guide outlines the procedure under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC) and related statutes:

1. **Preparation of Documents** – Collect birth certificates, HUF deed (if any), land records, tax receipts, and a family tree. These serve as evidence under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) Section 3 (formerly Evidence Act Section 3).
2. **Drafting the Plaint** – The plaint must state the plaintiff's status as a coparcener, describe the HUF property, and request a partition. Cite Section 6 (Amended) of HSA and Section 13 for the right to partition.
3. **Jurisdiction** – File the suit in the district court where the property is situated, as per Section 20 CPC.
4. **Service of Notice** – Under Order VI Rule 2 CPC, the court issues a notice to all coparceners. This initiates the conciliation process.
5. **Interim Relief** – If there is a risk of asset dissipation, the plaintiff may seek a temporary injunction under Order XXXIII Rule 1 CPC.
6. **Valuation and Survey** – The court may appoint a valuator under Section 34 CPC to assess the property.
7. **Decree of Partition** – Upon hearing, the court issues a decree dividing the property equally. The decree may direct registration of new titles with the local Sub‑Registrar.
8. **Execution** – If the opposite party resists, execution proceedings under Section 36 CPC can be initiated.

## Tax Implications and Post‑Partition Compliance

After a successful partition, the daughter becomes the legal owner of her share, triggering several tax and compliance obligations under Indian law.

2. **Stamp Duty** – Transfer of immovable property requires payment of stamp duty under the respective State Stamp Act. The duty is calculated on the market value declared in the partition decree.
3. **Registration** – Section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908 mandates registration of the transfer deed. Failure to register may affect enforceability against third parties.
4. **Self‑Assessment and Reporting** – The daughter must disclose the inherited share in her Income Tax Return under Schedule 'S' (Income from Capital Gains). Non‑disclosure may attract penalty under Section 270A of the Income Tax Act.
5. **Maintenance of Records** – Retain the partition decree, valuation report, and registration documents for at least six years, as required under the BSA for evidentiary purposes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do daughters have equal rights to ancestral property as sons?

Yes. Since the 2005 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act, daughters are coparceners with the same right to inherit and demand partition as sons.

### Can a daughter claim her share if the family has already divided the property?

If a valid partition deed exists, the daughter’s right is extinguished. She can challenge the deed only if it was executed without her consent or under duress.

### What is the limitation period to file a partition suit?

A partition suit must be filed within three years from the date the daughter’s right was denied, as per Section 14(2) of the Limitation Act, 1963.

### Do criminal charges apply if a male coparcener threatens a daughter?

Yes. Threats can be prosecuted under Section 506 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (formerly IPC Section 506) for criminal intimidation.
