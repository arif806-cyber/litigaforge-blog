# UK Child Maintenance 2026

In the UK, child maintenance is a critical aspect of family law, ensuring that children receive financial support from both parents. The Child Maintenance Service (CMS) plays a vital role in calculating, enforcing, and varying child maintenance payments, and understanding the process is essential for parents navigating the system in 2026.

## Introduction to Child Maintenance in the UK

In the UK, child maintenance is governed by the Child Support Act 1991 and the Child Maintenance and Other Payments Act 2008. The CMS is responsible for calculating and enforcing child maintenance payments, taking into account factors such as income, expenses, and the number of children involved. Parents can apply to the CMS for a maintenance calculation, which will determine the amount of maintenance payable. According to Section 11 of the Child Support Act 1991, the CMS must take into account the 'gross income' of the non-resident parent when calculating maintenance payments. The UK's Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) also provides guidance on child maintenance, emphasizing the importance of cooperation between parents to ensure the best possible outcome for children.

> **Key takeaway:** Parents can apply to the CMS for a maintenance calculation to determine the amount of maintenance payable.

## CMS Calculation: How is Child Maintenance Calculated in the UK?

The CMS calculation takes into account various factors, including the non-resident parent's gross income, the number of children involved, and the amount of time the children spend with each parent. The calculation is based on a percentage of the non-resident parent's income, with the following rates applying: 12% for one child, 16% for two children, and 19% for three or more children. According to the Child Maintenance and Other Payments Act 2008, Section 12, the CMS can also take into account 'special expenses' such as disability-related costs or education fees. The UK's Child Support Agency (CSA) also provides guidance on the calculation process, emphasizing the importance of accurate income information to ensure a fair maintenance assessment.

> **Key takeaway:** The CMS calculation is based on a percentage of the non-resident parent's income, taking into account the number of children and other factors.

## Enforcement of Child Maintenance Payments in the UK

If a non-resident parent fails to pay child maintenance, the CMS can take enforcement action to recover the debt. According to Section 32 of the Child Support Act 1991, the CMS can deduct payments from the non-resident parent's earnings, or apply for a liability order through the courts. The CMS can also impose penalties, such as a fine or a driving disqualification, for non-compliance. In cases where the non-resident parent has moved abroad, the CMS can work with international authorities to recover maintenance payments, as outlined in the Hague Convention on the International Recovery of Child Support and Other Forms of Family Maintenance 2007.

> **Key takeaway:** The CMS can take enforcement action to recover child maintenance debts, including deducting payments from earnings or applying for a liability order.

## Variation Requests: Changing Child Maintenance Payments in the UK

Either parent can request a variation to the child maintenance payment amount if there has been a significant change in circumstances. According to the Child Maintenance and Other Payments Act 2008, Section 28, a variation can be requested if there has been a change in income, a change in the number of children, or a change in the amount of time the children spend with each parent. The CMS will reassess the maintenance calculation based on the new circumstances, and may adjust the payment amount accordingly. The UK's Family Procedure Rules 2010, Part 33, also provide guidance on the variation process, emphasizing the importance of providing detailed evidence to support a variation request.

> **Key takeaway:** Either parent can request a variation to the child maintenance payment amount if there has been a significant change in circumstances.
