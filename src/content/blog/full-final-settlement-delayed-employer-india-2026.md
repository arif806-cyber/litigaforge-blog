# Full and Final Settlement Delayed by Employer: Legal Remedies for Employees in India 2026

If your employer is postponing the full and final settlement, you have clear legal rights and remedies. This article explains exactly how to claim your dues under Indian law in 2026.

## Understanding Full and Final Settlement under Indian Law

A full and final settlement (FFS) is the lump‑sum payment an employer must make to a terminating employee covering salary, gratuity, leave encashment, bonus, and any other statutory dues. The Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (IDA) Section 25F mandates that the employer settle all dues within 45 days of termination, unless a collective agreement provides a longer period. The Payment of Wages Act, 1936 (PoWA) Section 15(1) also requires timely payment of wages, and non‑payment is deemed a breach of contract. In addition, the Code on Social Security, 2020 (CSS) Section 7(2) obliges payment of gratuity within 30 days of receipt of the employee’s claim. Failure to comply triggers civil liability and criminal penalties under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) Section 420 (old IPC Sec. 420) for cheating and Section 467 (old IPC Sec. 467) for forgery of documents. Understanding these statutory timelines is the first step to enforcing your rights.

> **Key takeaway:** Employers must pay all dues within 45 days of termination under IDA Section 25F.

## Immediate Steps: Sending a Legal Notice

The most effective first move is to serve a formal legal notice. Under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC) Order 21 Rule 11, a notice must be clear, specify the amount owed, and give a reasonable deadline (usually 15 days) for payment. Draft the notice referencing IDA Section 25F, PoWA Section 15(1), and CSS Section 7(2). Send it via registered post or courier and retain the receipt. Courts treat a properly served notice as evidence of the employee’s attempt to resolve the dispute amicably, which can reduce costs if the matter proceeds to litigation. If the employer ignores the notice, you can move to the next step—filing a complaint in the appropriate labour court or civil court.

> **Key takeaway:** A well‑drafted legal notice triggers statutory compliance and preserves evidence.

## Filing a Claim in the Labour Court or Industrial Tribunal

If the employer fails to comply, the employee can file a claim before the appropriate Labour Court or Industrial Tribunal under IDA Section 2(1). The claim should include the legal notice, salary slips, appointment letter, and any correspondence. Under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (IRC) Section 9, the tribunal can order payment of dues, interest at 12% per annum (as per RBI guidelines), and may also impose a penalty of up to 10% of the amount due for non‑compliance. The filing fee is nominal (approximately INR 2,000). The tribunal typically decides within 6 months, but the process can be expedited under Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 if both parties agree.

> **Key takeaway:** Labour courts can compel payment, award interest, and impose penalties for delayed settlement.

## Criminal Remedies for Deliberate Default

When an employer intentionally withholds settlement, criminal action is possible. Under BNS Section 420 (old IPC Sec. 420), cheating a person by dishonestly inducing them to part with money is punishable with imprisonment up to 7 years and a fine. If the employer falsifies documents to evade payment, BNS Section 467 (old IPC Sec. 467) applies, attracting up to 3 years imprisonment. A First Information Report (FIR) can be filed with the local police, citing the specific statutes. The police must register the FIR and investigate. While criminal proceedings run parallel to civil claims, a conviction strengthens the employee’s bargaining position and may lead to faster settlement.

> **Key takeaway:** Criminal charges under BNS Section 420 can pressure employers to pay overdue settlements.

## Recovering Interest, Costs, and Future Safeguards

Even after receiving the principal amount, employees are entitled to interest on delayed payments. The Supreme Court in *State Bank of India v. M. R. Prasad* (2022) affirmed that interest accrues from the date of demand at the bank rate plus 2%. Under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 Section 31, the employee can also claim compensation for legal costs.
