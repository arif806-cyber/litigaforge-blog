# Contest a Will UK 2026

If you believe a will does not reflect the true intentions of the deceased due to undue influence, lack of capacity, or other issues, you may be able to contest it. In the UK, the process of contesting a will involves understanding the grounds for contesting, such as those outlined under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 and the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975.

## Grounds for Contesting a Will

To contest a will in the UK, you must have valid grounds. These grounds include undue influence, lack of capacity, fraud, forgery, or lack of proper execution as per the Wills Act 1837. Undue influence, as defined in cases like Edwards v. Edwards (2008), occurs when the deceased was pressured into making a will that does not reflect their true wishes. Lack of capacity, on the other hand, refers to situations where the deceased did not have the mental ability to make a will, as outlined in the Mental Capacity Act 2005. For instance, Section 2 of the Mental Capacity Act 2005 states that a person lacks capacity if they are unable to make a decision for themselves due to an impairment of, or a disturbance in the functioning of, the mind or brain.

> **Key takeaway:** Identifying the correct grounds for contesting a will is crucial for a successful claim.

## Inheritance Act Claims

The Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975 allows certain individuals to make a claim against the estate if they believe the will does not make reasonable provision for them. These individuals include the spouse or civil partner of the deceased, former spouses or civil partners who have not remarried, children, or individuals who were maintained by the deceased. To make a claim, you must apply to the court within six months of the grant of probate or letters of administration, as per Section 4 of the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975. The court will consider factors such as the applicant's financial resources and the size of the estate when determining whether to make an order.

> **Key takeaway:** Making an Inheritance Act claim requires swift action and a clear understanding of your entitlements under the law.

## Procedure for Contesting a Will

The process of contesting a will involves several steps, starting with gathering evidence to support your claim. This can include witness statements, medical records, and any other relevant documents. You should then seek legal advice from a solicitor who specializes in will disputes. Your solicitor can help you understand the legal basis for your claim and guide you through the process of making a formal challenge to the will. This may involve entering a caveat to prevent the grant of probate or making a claim under the Inheritance Act. According to the Civil Procedure Rules 1998, specifically Part 57, the procedure for contesting a will involves specific court proceedings.

> **Key takeaway:** Seeking professional legal advice is essential for navigating the complex process of contesting a will.

## Undue Influence and Lack of Capacity

Undue influence and lack of capacity are two of the most common grounds for contesting a will. Undue influence occurs when someone coerces the deceased into making a will that they would not have otherwise made. Lack of capacity, as mentioned, refers to the deceased not having the mental ability to understand the implications of their will. In cases of suspected undue influence, the court may consider factors such as the nature of the relationship between the deceased and the person alleged to have exerted influence, as seen in the case of Schomberg v. Taylor (2013). For lack of capacity, medical evidence and testimony from individuals who knew the deceased can be crucial in establishing that the deceased did not have the requisite mental capacity at the time of making the will.

> **Key takeaway:** Establishing undue influence or lack of capacity requires strong evidence and a thorough understanding of the legal standards.

## Timeline and Penalties

The timeline for contesting a will is critical. Generally, a claim under the Inheritance Act must be made within six months of the grant of probate. For other types of will contests, such as those based on undue influence or lack of capacity, the limitation period can vary. It is essential to act promptly to avoid missing the deadline. Penalties for failing to do so can include the dismissal of your claim. Furthermore, if your claim is found to be without merit, you may be liable for the legal costs of the other parties involved, as per the Civil Procedure Rules 1998. Therefore, it is crucial to have a solid legal basis for your claim before proceeding.

> **Key takeaway:** Understanding the deadlines and potential penalties for contesting a will is vital to ensure your claim is not jeopardized.
