# Singapore Employment Pass 2026: COMPASS Framework, Salary Criteria & Appeal Guide

The 2026 Employment Pass (EP) in Singapore hinges on the new COMPASS framework and stricter salary criteria, and many applicants wonder how to overcome a rejection. This guide explains the legal requirements, common pitfalls and the step‑by‑step appeal process.

## Understanding the COMPASS Framework under the Employment Pass Act

The Employment Pass (EP) is governed by the Employment Pass Act (Cap. 91) and the Immigration (Amendment) Regulations 2025, which introduced the COMPASS framework effective 1 January 2026. COMPASS is an acronym for: C – Credential verification, O – Occupational relevance, M – Minimum salary, P – Professional experience, A – Ability to contribute, S – Security clearance, S – Sponsorship by an approved employer. 

1. Credential verification requires that the applicant hold a recognized diploma, degree or professional qualification that is comparable to a Singaporean university degree. The Ministry of Manpower (MOM) cross‑checks qualifications against the International Credential Evaluation Service (ICES). 

2. Occupational relevance means the job must fall under one of the eligible categories listed in Annex A of the Immigration (Amendment) Regulations 2025, such as finance, engineering, ICT, or senior management. 

3. Minimum salary is the most scrutinised element. As of 2026, the baseline salary is SGD 5,000 per month for fresh graduates and SGD 6,500 for experienced professionals, with higher thresholds for senior roles (e.g., SGD 12,000 for managerial positions). The salary must be the actual gross monthly remuneration, inclusive of allowances, but exclusive of bonuses that are not guaranteed. 

4. Professional experience requires at least three years of relevant work experience for mid‑level positions; senior roles demand five years or more, documented through employment letters and tax records. 

5. Ability to contribute assesses the applicant’s potential impact on the Singaporean economy, measured by factors such as innovation, revenue generation, or skill transfer. 

6. Security clearance involves a background check under the Immigration Act (Cap. 274) Section 7, ensuring the applicant does not pose a security risk. 

7. Sponsorship mandates that the employer be a registered entity with a valid Employment Pass quota and must submit a formal sponsorship letter on MOM’s e‑Submission portal. Failure in any COMPASS pillar results in automatic rejection, but the applicant may address deficiencies through the appeal process outlined later. 

Compliance with the COMPASS framework is not optional; it is a statutory prerequisite under Section 5 of the Employment Pass Act, and non‑compliance can lead to a fine of up to SGD 10,000 per violation.

> **Key takeaway:** Ensure every COMPASS pillar is fully satisfied before submission to avoid automatic rejection.

## Salary Criteria: How Much Is Enough in 2026?

The salary threshold for the EP is the cornerstone of the 2026 reforms. Section 6(2) of the Employment Pass Act empowers MOM to set a minimum qualifying salary, which is reviewed annually. For 2026, the official rates are: 

- Fresh graduate (0‑2 years experience): SGD 5,000 per month 
- Mid‑level professional (3‑5 years): SGD 6,500 per month 
- Senior manager/executive (6+ years): SGD 12,000 per month 

These figures are inclusive of fixed allowances (e.g., housing, transport) but exclude variable components such as performance bonuses, commissions, or stock options unless they are guaranteed under the contract. 

Employers must provide a salary certificate on MOM’s EP Online portal, signed by an authorized signatory, stating the exact gross monthly salary. If the salary falls below the threshold, the employer can still apply under the “Specialist Pass” route, but this requires a separate endorsement under Section 9 of the Employment Pass Act and is limited to 5% of the total EP quota. 

Penalties for misrepresenting salary include revocation of the EP under Section 12(1) and a fine up to SGD 20,000 per false declaration, as per the Immigration (Control and Penalties) Regulations 2025. Moreover, the employer may face a suspension of its quota for up to six months under Section 14 of the same Act. 

Practical tip: When budgeting for EP hires, factor in a 10% buffer above the statutory minimum to accommodate future salary increments and to demonstrate the applicant’s contribution to the company’s growth, which strengthens the “Ability to contribute” COMPASS pillar.

> **Key takeaway:** Offer a salary at least 10% above the statutory minimum to safeguard against future adjustments and strengthen the EP case.

## Common Grounds for EP Rejection and How to Remedy Them

MOM issues a rejection letter citing specific grounds under Section 8 of the Employment Pass Act. The most frequent reasons in 2026 are: 

1. Insufficient salary – the offered salary falls below the COMPASS threshold. Remedy: Submit a revised salary offer with a new employment contract and a salary certificate. 
2. Inadequate qualifications – the degree is not recognised or does not meet the credential requirement. Remedy: Provide an accredited equivalency letter from ICES or attach additional professional certifications. 
3. Occupational mismatch – the job title does not align with the eligible occupations list. Remedy: Re‑classify the role to a recognised occupation and update the job description accordingly. 
4. Lack of employer quota – the sponsoring company has exhausted its EP quota. Remedy: Apply for a quota increase via MOM’s “Quota Allocation Request” (Form QAR) under Section 11 of the Employment Pass Act, providing evidence of business growth and need for talent. 
5. Incomplete documentation – missing forms, signatures or supporting evidence. Remedy: Resubmit a complete dossier within 14 days, attaching a checklist as per MOM’s EP Application Guide 2026. 

Each rejection can be appealed (see next section). It is crucial to address the exact reason cited; generic amendments will not satisfy MOM’s scrutiny. Additionally, maintain a record of all communications on the EP Online portal, as MOM may request further clarification within 7 days of the appeal submission.

> **Key takeaway:** Match the appeal precisely to the rejection reason and provide complete, verifiable documentation.

## The Appeal Process: Step‑by‑Step Guide

When an EP application is rejected, the applicant (or employer) may file an appeal under Section 15 of the Employment Pass Act within 14 calendar days of the rejection date. The appeal procedure is as follows: 

1. **Prepare the Appeal Letter** – Draft a concise letter addressed to the Director of Immigration, citing the rejection reference number, and clearly stating the grounds for appeal. Include a table summarising the original submission and the corrective actions taken. 
2. **Attach Supporting Documents** – Provide revised salary certificates, updated qualification equivalency letters, amended job descriptions, and any additional evidence that addresses the rejection grounds. 
3. **Submit via EP Online Portal** – Upload the appeal package using the “Appeal EP Rejection” function. The system generates a receipt with an appeal tracking number. 
4. **MOM Review** – MOM has up to 30 days to render a decision. During this period, they may request further information under Section 16(2). Respond within 7 days to avoid dismissal of the appeal. 
5. **Decision** – If approved, MOM issues an EP In-Principle Approval (IPA) valid for 30 days, after which the applicant must collect the actual EP card. If denied, the applicant may seek judicial review in the High Court under the Administrative Law Act 1979, but only after exhausting internal remedies. 

Costs: No additional filing fee is required for the appeal, but the employer must bear any legal counsel fees. Failure to appeal within the 14‑day window results in loss of the opportunity to re‑apply under the same quota, forcing a fresh application and a new quota allocation request. 

Practical tip: Engage a qualified immigration consultant to draft the appeal letter, ensuring legal terminology aligns with MOM’s regulatory language, which increases the likelihood of success.

> **Key takeaway:** File the appeal within 14 days with a precise, document‑backed response to MOM’s rejection points.

## Impact of Indian and UAE Laws on Cross‑Border EP Applicants

Many EP candidates are Indian or UAE nationals, and their home‑country regulations can affect the application. 

- **India**: The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) 2024, Section 123, criminalises providing false documents abroad. Applicants must ensure that any Indian educational certificates are attested by the Ministry of External Affairs and the Singapore High Commission. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) Section 45 requires Indian citizens to inform the Ministry of Home Affairs before accepting overseas employment, failing which a penalty of INR 50,000 may be imposed. 

- **UAE**: The UAE Labour Law (Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021) mandates that employees obtain a No‑Objection Certificate (NOC) from their current employer before resigning. Without an NOC, the employee risks a breach of contract claim under Article 119, which can lead to a civil suit in UAE courts. Additionally, the UAE’s Immigration and Passports Authority requires an exit permit for expatriates leaving the country, per Ministerial Decision No. 45/2025. 

Compliance with these home‑country requirements prevents future legal challenges and ensures the Singapore EP process proceeds smoothly. Applicants should retain copies of all attestations, NOCs, and exit permits, and upload them as supplementary documents in the EP appeal if relevant.

**Penalties**: Non‑compliance with BNS Section 123 may attract imprisonment up to 6 months, while breach of UAE Labour Law can result in damages equal to three months’ salary as per Article 121. Both jurisdictions recognise the Singapore EP as a foreign work permit, so cross‑border legal obligations remain enforceable.

> **Key takeaway:** Secure all home‑country clearances and attestations before filing the Singapore EP to avoid criminal or civil penalties abroad.
