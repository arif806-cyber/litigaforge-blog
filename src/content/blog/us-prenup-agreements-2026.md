## What is a Prenuptial Agreement?

A prenuptial agreement, also known as a prenup, is a contract between two individuals planning to get married, outlining the division of assets, property, and financial responsibilities in the event of a divorce or death. In the US, the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA) provides a framework for the creation and enforcement of prenups. According to the UPAA, a prenup must be in writing, signed by both parties, and notarized to be considered valid. For instance, Section 3 of the UPAA states that a prenup is not enforceable if the party against whom enforcement is sought proves that they did not execute the agreement voluntarily. The enforceability of prenuptial agreements in the US is governed by state laws, which vary from state to state. ## How Courts Review Prenuptial Agreements

When reviewing a prenuptial agreement, courts consider various factors to determine its validity and enforceability. These factors include: 1) whether the agreement was executed voluntarily, 2) whether the agreement is fair and reasonable, 3) whether the agreement is in writing and signed by both parties, and 4) whether the agreement provides for the basic needs of both parties. In the case of In re Marriage of Bonds, 24 Cal.4th 1 (2000), the California Supreme Court held that a prenup is enforceable if it is fair and reasonable at the time of execution. Similarly, the UK's Radmacher v. Granatino [2010] UKSC 42 case established that prenuptial agreements can be enforceable if they are fair and meet the basic needs of both parties.

> **Key takeaway:** Courts consider factors such as voluntariness, fairness, and reasonableness when reviewing the validity and enforceability of a prenup.
